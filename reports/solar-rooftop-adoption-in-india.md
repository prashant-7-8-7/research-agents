# Solar rooftop adoption in India

# Summary
Solar rooftop adoption in India is influenced by a combination of technological, economic, and policy factors. The commercial and industrial sectors have historically driven adoption due to favorable economic conditions. However, residential adoption has seen significant growth, particularly with the implementation of the Pradhan Mantri Surya Ghar Yojana (PMSGY). Government policies, such as the Grid Connected Rooftop Solar Programme, provide financial and institutional support. Despite these efforts, financial and regulatory barriers, along with awareness and policy changes, continue to pose challenges. Local government policies have shown varying levels of success, with some states achieving higher adoption rates than others.

# Key Findings

## Factors Influencing Solar Rooftop Adoption
Several factors influence solar rooftop adoption in India. These include technology readiness, attitudes, and purchase intentions [1], as well as macro-environmental factors. The commercial and industrial sectors have historically driven adoption due to favorable economic conditions such as higher electricity tariffs and access to institutional credit [3]. Additionally, policy incentives, such as the Pradhan Mantri Surya Ghar Yojana (PMSGY), have significantly boosted residential adoption, nearly doubling the growth rate from 45% to 85% [4, 5]. Environmental and economic factors, along with knowledge levels and perceived obstacles, also play crucial roles in residential adoption [5].

## Government Policies and Incentives
The Indian government, through the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE), has implemented the Grid Connected Rooftop Solar Programme to achieve a cumulative installed capacity of 40,000 MW from grid-connected rooftop solar projects by 2026 [7]. Under this programme, central financial assistance (CFA) is provided to residential consumers under Component-A, with a target of 1 crore households [9]. Consumers can apply for installation through either the National Portal for Rooftop Solar or State DISCOMs portal [7]. The programme also includes incentives for distribution companies (DISCOMs) under Component-B for the initial 18 GW capacity [7]. For residential consumers, the system size is practically limited by the sanctioned load under most state distribution company (DISCOM) net metering regulations, with a maximum subsidy of Rs. 78,000 for the first 3 kW under the PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana [9].

## Challenges Faced in the Adoption of Solar Rooftops
The adoption of solar rooftops in India faces several challenges, primarily financial and regulatory barriers [2, 13, 16]. Market-based and financial barriers are the most significant, followed by institutional and regulatory, technical, and location-based barriers [2]. Additionally, uneven district-level adoption, with some urban areas exceeding targets while others lag behind, highlights the importance of local governance and municipal readiness [15]. Awareness among consumers and frequent policy changes also pose significant hurdles [17]. To overcome these challenges, the government has implemented various policies and schemes, but more consistent regulations and improved financing models are needed [16].

## Case Studies of Successful Solar Rooftop Projects
Several successful solar rooftop projects in India are highlighted in the case studies provided. For instance, Tata Power Solar has implemented a 10.8 MW rooftop solar power system for ANERT in Kerala [20]. Additionally, residential and commercial projects such as a 5.25 kW solar system for Suvidha Housing Society in Bengaluru and a 820.8 kWp installation for CCI Stadium in Mumbai are also notable [20]. These projects demonstrate the practical application of solar rooftops in various settings, contributing to India's renewable energy goals [19].

## Financial and Regulatory Barriers
Consumers in India face several financial and regulatory barriers in adopting solar rooftops. Financially, high upfront costs and limited loan accessibility remain significant hurdles [3, 25]. Regulatory barriers include DISCOM resistance due to potential revenue loss, varied installation approval processes across states, and frequent changes in net metering regulations [17, 25]. Additionally, informational barriers such as consumer unawareness of government subsidies and solar benefits also contribute to slower adoption rates [17, 25].

## Local Government Policies
Local government policies have played a significant role in influencing the adoption rate of solar rooftops in India [28, 29, 3, 14]. States have developed solar policies and distributed renewable energy policies, setting targets and providing incentives, which have helped in enabling rooftop solar programmes [28]. However, the progress has been incremental, and adoption rates have been sluggish, despite a deliberate policy push since 2014 [14]. These policies, along with enabling regulations, are crucial for the deployment of rooftop solar systems [28, 29]. Nonetheless, structural and implementation inefficiencies in the policy landscape continue to pose challenges [27].

# Open Questions

2. What specific measures can be taken to improve the financing models for solar rooftop installations to make them more accessible to a wider range of consumers?
3. How can local governments ensure more consistent and effective implementation of solar policies to overcome structural and regulatory challenges?
